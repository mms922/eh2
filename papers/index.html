Alberto Alesina, Paola Giuliano, Nathan Nunn On the Origins of Gender Roles: Women and the PloughQuarterly Journal of Economics, 2013 Appendix Societies whose ancestors farmed with the plough, which needs upper-body strength, developed a stricter split between men's and women's work. Their descendants, including children of immigrants in Europe and the US, still hold less equal views on women working.
Marcella Alsan The Effect of the TseTse Fly on African DevelopmentAmerican Economic Review, 2015 The tsetse fly, found only in Africa, kills cattle. Where the climate suited it, Africans kept fewer animals, rarely used the plough, farmed less intensively and formed fewer centralised states, with effects still visible today.
Philipp Barteska, Jay Euijung Lee The Implementers of Industrial Policy: Bureaucrats and the Korean Export MiracleWorking paper, 2026 South Korea's export offices abroad rotated their managers on a fixed schedule. Comparing the same office under different managers shows that who ran it mattered as much as opening it.
Brian Beach, W. Walker Hanlon Culture and the Historical Fertility TransitionReview of Economic Studies, 2023 In 1877 a London obscenity trial over a contraception pamphlet made birth control front-page news. British fertility started falling that very year, and so did the fertility of British-origin families in Canada and elsewhere, but not their neighbours'.
Sebastian Till Braun, Richard Franke Railways, Growth, and Industrialization in a Developing German Economy, 1829–1910Journal of Economic History, 2022 The first railway lines reached Württemberg in the 1840s and 1850s. Towns on the line grew faster, paid higher wages and adopted steam engines, compared with towns on routes that were proposed but never built. Most of the gains went to places that already had some industry.
Leonardo Bursztyn, Davide Cantoni A Tear in the Iron Curtain: The Impact of Western Television on Consumption BehaviorReview of Economics and Statistics, 2016 Some East Germans could watch West German TV and its ads; others, in valleys or far from transmitters, could not. After reunification, those who had watched spent no more overall, but bought more of the products that had been advertised.
Davide Cantoni Adopting a New Religion: The Case of Protestantism in 16th Century GermanyEconomic Journal, 2012 Why did some German territories adopt Lutheranism in the 1500s and others not? Distance to Wittenberg mattered a lot, and lords were more likely to convert once powerful neighbours had, because switching religion was risky.
Christoffer Cappelen, Jacob Gerner Hariri The State and the Monopolization of Violence: Castles in Europe, 1000–1700Comparative Political Studies, 2026 How did European rulers come to monopolise violence? The authors trace who owned 5,400 castles in 17 countries from 1000 to 1700, and use the Crown's growing share as a measure of state formation.
David de la Croix, Mara Vitale Women in European academia before 1800: religion, marriage, and human capitalEuropean Review of Economic History, 2023 Who were the 108 women who taught at universities or sat in academies in Europe before 1800? Mostly in the Catholic south, not the Protestant north; often childless; and on average more prominent than their male colleagues, which suggests discrimination.
Jeremiah Dittmar, Ralf Meisenzahl Public Goods Institutions, Human Capital, and Growth: Evidence from German HistoryReview of Economic Studies, 2020 In the 1500s some German cities passed Protestant church laws that set up public schools and poor relief. Those cities then attracted and produced more highly educated people, and grew faster up to 1800.
Alan Fernihough, Kevin Hjortshøj O'Rourke Coal and the European Industrial RevolutionEconomic Journal, 2021 How much did coal matter for the Industrial Revolution? Across 2,000 European cities, being near coal made no difference before 1750 and a large one afterwards, once steam technology could use it.
Vasiliki Fouka Backlash: The Unintended Effects of Language Prohibition in U.S. Schools after World War IReview of Economic Studies, 2020 After World War I, many US states banned German in schools. German-American children exposed to the bans grew up less assimilated: they married within the group more, gave their children German names more often and volunteered less in World War II.
Claudia Goldin The Role of World War II in the Rise of Women's EmploymentAmerican Economic Review, 1991 Did World War II's "Rosie the Riveter" push married women into work for good? Goldin finds that more than half of the women who entered work during the war had left by 1950, and most women working in 1950 already worked before it.
Stephan Heblich, Alex Trew, Yanos Zylberberg East-Side Story: Historical Pollution and Persistent Neighborhood SortingJournal of Political Economy, 2021 Factory smoke in Victorian English cities blew east with the prevailing wind, so better-off families settled upwind and the poor were left downwind. These east-west differences survive today, long after the smoke stopped.
Richard Hornbeck, Suresh Naidu When the Levee Breaks: Black Migration and Economic Development in the American SouthAmerican Economic Review, 2014 The 1927 Mississippi flood pushed Black workers out of flooded counties for good. Landowners there then mechanised, which suggests that cheap, captive Black labour had held back the modernisation of Southern farming.
Adam Izdebski et al. Palaeoecological data indicates land-use changes across Europe linked to spatial heterogeneity in mortality during the Black Death pandemicNature Ecology & Evolution, 2022 Did the Black Death kill half of Europe everywhere? Pollen preserved at 261 sites shows where farmland was abandoned after 1350. Mortality varied enormously: some regions collapsed, others kept growing.
Remi Jedwab, Noel D. Johnson, Mark Koyama Pandemics and Cities: Evidence from the Black Death and the Long-RunJournal of Urban Economics, 2024 The Black Death killed far more people in some European cities than in others, largely by luck of timing. Did the worst-hit cities stay small? Most recovered within about two centuries, mainly through migration, and cities on coasts, rivers and trade routes recovered fastest.
Morgan Kelly, Cormac Ó Gráda Adam Smith, Watch Prices, and the Industrial RevolutionQuarterly Journal of Economics, 2016 Adam Smith claimed that watch prices had collapsed in the century before 1776. Using 3,200 stolen watches valued in London's Old Bailey trials, the authors find steep, steady price falls: sustained innovation started well before the Industrial Revolution.
Morgan Kelly, Joel Mokyr, Cormac Ó Gráda The Mechanics of the Industrial RevolutionJournal of Political Economy, 2023 Why did some English counties industrialise between the 1760s and the 1830s? Not because of coal, literacy or banks, the authors argue, but because they had many skilled mechanics and low wages.
William F. Maloney, Felipe Valencia Caicedo Engineering GrowthJournal of the European Economic Association, 2022 Around 1900, countries with similar incomes had very different numbers of engineers: many in the US and Scandinavia, few in Latin America. Places with more engineers grew richer over the next century, which the authors read as a greater ability to adopt new technology. Cases include mining in Chile, Mexico and Minas Gerais.
Grant Miller Women's Suffrage, Political Responsiveness, and Child Survival in American HistoryQuarterly Journal of Economics, 2008 US states gave women the vote at different times between 1869 and 1920. Right after each state did, politicians spent more on public health, and deaths of children from infectious diseases fell.
Santiago Pérez Southern (American) Hospitality: Italians in Argentina and the United States During the Age of Mass MigrationEconomic Journal, 2021 Italians who migrated around 1900 did better in Argentina than in the US: more owned homes and fewer were stuck in unskilled jobs. Not because better migrants chose Argentina, but because Italians fitted better there, in language and in skills relative to locals.
Akiva Sanders Fingerprints, sex, state, and the organization of the Tell Leilan ceramic industryJournal of Archaeological Science, 2015 Fingerprints left on ancient pottery reveal who made it. Before the state formed in northern Mesopotamia (around 2600 BCE), both men and women made pots. Afterwards, city potters were almost all men, while nearby villages stayed mixed.
Carol H. Shiue, Wolfgang Keller Markets in China and Europe on the Eve of the Industrial RevolutionAmerican Economic Review, 2007 Was Europe ahead of China in market development before the Industrial Revolution? Grain prices from 121 Chinese prefectures and from European cities show that around 1770 China's markets worked about as well as Western Europe's. Only England was ahead.
Nathan Sussman, Yishay Yafeh Institutional Reforms, Financial Development and Sovereign Debt: Britain 1690–1790Journal of Economic History, 2006 North and Weingast argued that the Glorious Revolution of 1688 made Britain's government credible, so its borrowing costs fell. The data say otherwise: rates stayed high and volatile for decades, driven by wars, and fell only from around 1720.
